This is a simplified map and the fields overlap in practice.

  • Artificial intelligence: the broad field of systems performing tasks associated with human intelligence: perception, language, reasoning, learning, or decision-making. A virtual assistant combines several of these.
  • Machine learning: systems learn patterns from examples rather than having every rule explicitly programmed. Example: spam filtering.
  • Supervised learning: labeled examples include the input and correct answer (e.g. emails labeled spam or safe).
  • Unsupervised learning: discovers structure without predefined labels (e.g. customer segmentation/clustering).
  • Reinforcement learning: an agent learns via rewards and penalties (e.g. game strategies).
  • Deep learning: multi-layered neural networks learning complex representations from large data.
  • Computer vision: extracts meaning from images/video.
  • Speech processing: recognizes, understands, or produces speech.
  • Generative AI: creates new content from learned patterns.
  • Image/video models: generate or transform visual content.
  • LLMs: models trained on text/code to predict token sequences.
  • Robotics: combines decisions with sensors/actuators in physical world.
  • Planning/search: explores states/actions toward a goal.
  • Expert systems: explicit human-written rules.

LLMs are neural networks based on the Transformer architecture. During training they repeatedly predict the next token. Predicting well forces them to learn statistical representations of grammar, concepts, facts, relationships, code structure, and common reasoning patterns.

The model's knowledge is encoded statistically across billions of parameters. It is closer to a lossy compression of patterns in human knowledge than a verified database. Current limitations
11

Capability is uneven

Plausible
≠ correct
HallucinationLimited groundingFinite contextNon-determinism Prompt sensitivityLong-task driftUnreliable confidenceBiasOutdated knowledgeSecurity risks Verification gaps
  • Hallucination, largely intrinsic: next-token prediction rewards plausible language, not verified truth; a model can invent a library method and convincing docs.
  • Limited grounding, intrinsic to text-only training: words describe reality, but the model has not physically experienced it.
  • Finite context, architectural/operational: only available context can be reasoned over, and included details may still be overlooked.
  • Non-determinism, usually intentional: sampling means the same prompt can yield correct or faulty SQL.
  • Prompt sensitivity, largely intrinsic: “fix this” may rewrite broadly; “smallest change passing this test” may target precisely.
  • Long-task drift, partly intrinsic/systemic: generated actions become later context, early errors compound, and no inherent persistent goal state exists.
  • Unreliable confidence: token probability measures plausible wording, not truth; confident regex claims may fail Unicode.
  • Training-data errors/bias: imperfect human text/code can promote outdated insecure patterns.
  • Outdated knowledge, not fundamental: weights are mostly fixed after training; retrieval/tools can address it.
  • Specification dependence: “make login faster” may remove implicit security controls.
  • Verification gaps: passing tests cannot validate untested concurrency.
  • Security risks: instructions and untrusted content share a natural-language channel; malicious README/issue content may induce secret exfiltration. Restrict permissions.
  • Opaque reasoning: distributed numerical computation makes precise internal reasons difficult to inspect.
  • Cost/latency: repeated repository reads, tool calls, and full tests can cost minutes and millions of tokens.

Models can improve through better training data, larger or more efficient architectures, improved reasoning, longer context, multimodal input, stronger post-training, and better uncertainty calibration. The engineering contract

Ground important claims.
Verify consequential outputs.

Never turn fluent output into automatic trust.

EvidenceTestsReviewControls

Open-ended generation is optimized for plausible output rather than truth. Better models can reduce hallucinations, but unrestricted answers are never automatically correct. Reliable systems ground important claims in evidence and verify consequential outputs. Current LLMs also lack human experience, persistent goals, and consistently grounded physical/social understanding. Their capabilities are real and remarkable but uneven: a model may solve a difficult coding problem and fail a simple everyday situation.

The goal is not to know everything first.

This young field has significant potential impact and investment. Models, tools, and practices improve quickly, so developers should make a reasonable effort to stay informed to recognize opportunities, adapt, and remain competitive.

Do not follow every release, feature, benchmark, or social post. That creates anxiety, burnout, and FOMO, and much of today's excitement will be replaced or forgotten. Review periodically, experiment selectively, and focus on developments that remain useful after the initial hype. Reasonable occasional investment will catch advances that matter once the dust settles. The goal is not to know everything first; it is to recognize durable changes and apply them effectively when relevant.

Intellectual property
28

Separate the three IP questions.

01

03

Separate three questions often confused: Input authority: are we permitted to disclose and process the supplied code, data, document, or image? Output ownership: is the generated result protectable, and who owns any rights? Freedom to use: could using or distributing it infringe copyright, a license, a patent, a trademark, a trade secret, or a contract?

A vendor assigning output rights does not guarantee that the output is copyrightable or free of third-party rights.

Do not submit company, customer, open-source, or third-party material unless its ownership, license, confidentiality terms, and permitted recipients allow it. A promise not to train on the material does not create permission to disclose it. Trade-secret protection depends partly on reasonable measures to preserve secrecy. Sending confidential code to an unapproved service can breach contractual duties and weaken trade-secret protection.

Treat generated code as having unknown provenance. Investigate output containing a license or copyright notice; distinctive comments, names, strings, or test data; a long block closely resembling a known project; or a public-code match or citation reported by the tool.

AI does not remove an upstream open-source license. If generated output incorporates protected licensed code, obligations such as attribution, notices, source availability, or copyleft may still apply. “Permissive” does not mean “no obligations,” and there is no universal safe number of matching lines.

When provenance is suspicious: do not merge the output. Search distinctive fragments and inspect the original repository and license. Run the organization's normal composition and license checks. Satisfy the obligations, obtain Legal guidance, or independently implement from the requirements without using the suspect text.

Patent and trademark risks are separate. Independently generated code can still practice a patent, and generated product names or logos can still infringe trademarks. Escalate commercially significant or market-facing cases rather than relying on a model answer.

Two opposite mistakes are possible: using one agent for work that benefits from multiple specialized or parallel agents, and adding multiple agents where one coherent agent is simpler and safer. Choose based on whether the work is divisible, independent, and easy to reconcile.

Match repository scope too. A coding agent confined to a single repository is the wrong setup when the task spans multiple related repositories; where appropriate, give it a monorepo or multi-repository workspace containing the required context. Finally, do not use a coding agent without the necessary skills or tools for the job. Access to the right model is not enough: the harness needs relevant files, commands, browser/research capabilities, integrations, permissions, and verification tools.

Sometimes the right choice is not to use an AI tool at all. If you repeatedly ask an AI agent, “What is the command to port-forward this service port in Kubernetes to this local port?”, repeated inference is slower and more expensive than durable automation.

Use the agent once to create a small, reviewed shell function, then invoke that deterministic function directly. The demo shows this workflow. General rule: use AI for ambiguity and creation; once a repeated task becomes stable and well understood, encode it in the simplest conventional tool that works.

Hands-on exercise
42
Your turn

Create your own script
for quick port-forwarding.

Turn a repeated Kubernetes workflow into a small, reusable terminal tool.

Exercise

Make the routine one command.

Build a script that helps you select a service, choose a port, start forwarding, and open the local URL.

Create a reviewed Bash script for a quick Kubernetes service port-forward workflow. The script should: 1. Accept an optional service name argument. If none is provided, list services in the active namespace and let the user choose one. 2. Discover the active namespace with kubectl config view --minify and use default when no namespace is configured. 3. Use gum filter for fuzzy, sorted single-choice service selection. Use gum choose when a service exposes multiple ports. Check the exit status after each interactive selection so Ctrl+C or cancellation exits cleanly. 4. Query the selected service with kubectl and extract its exposed service ports. Use jsonpath or custom-columns where sufficient; jq is optional. 5. Allocate an unused local TCP port by briefly binding a socket to port 0, for example with Python, then closing the socket before starting the forward. 6. Start kubectl port-forward in the background using service/<service> and the mapping local-port:service-port. Capture the process ID and print a clear stop command. 7. Print http://localhost:<local-port>, copy the URL to the clipboard when supported, and open it with xdg-open on Linux or open on macOS. Fall back to printing the URL. 8. Add a Ctrl+C trap, quote shell variables safely, validate required commands, and explain the small race between choosing a free port and kubectl binding it. 9. Document dependencies: Bash, kubectl, gum for interactive selection, and Python 3 for dynamic port allocation. Treat pbcopy, xdg-open, open, and jq as optional. Ask the agent to explain the design, generate the script, and include a short verification plan. Inspect the result before testing it in a safe Kubernetes context.

Participants do not need access to any private reference file. This hint is the distilled design of the demonstrated workflow.

The key idea is to use gum for the human-friendly terminal UI: gum filter provides fuzzy service selection, while gum choose handles a short list of exposed ports. The script still needs to check each command's exit status so cancellation is treated as a normal, clean exit.

The underlying logic is: determine the Kubernetes namespace, select a service, query its service ports, select a port when necessary, obtain a free local port, start kubectl port-forward in the background, capture its PID, print and optionally open the local URL, and tell the user how to stop the process.

The reference workflow uses Bash, kubectl, gum, Python socket binding, and platform-specific clipboard/browser commands. jq is not essential to that logic; participants can use kubectl jsonpath or custom-columns instead, or choose jq if it makes their implementation clearer.

Have participants ask the agent to explain the design, generate the script, and provide a verification plan. They must inspect the generated script and test only in a safe Kubernetes context. This exercise is about turning a repeated prompt into durable automation, not running unknown commands against production.

Real-world example · PocketOS
46

A routine task.
A permanent loss.

The agent was working on a routine task in our staging environment. It encountered a credential mismatch and decided — entirely on its own initiative — to “fix” the problem by deleting a Railway volume.

To execute the deletion, the agent went looking for an API token. It found one in a file completely unrelated to the task it was working on. That token had been created for one purpose: to add and remove custom domains via the Railway CLI for our services. We had no idea — and Railway's token-creation flow gave us no warning — that the same token had blanket authority across the entire Railway GraphQL API, including destructive operations like volumeDelete. Had we known a CLI token created for routine domain operations could also delete production volumes, we would never have stored it.

curl -X POST https://backboard.railway.app/graphql/v2 \ -H "Authorization: Bearer [token]" \ -d '{"query":"mutation { volumeDelete(volumeId: \"3d2c42fb-...\") }"}'

There was no confirmation step. No “type DELETE to confirm.” No “this volume contains production data, are you sure?” No environment scoping. Nothing.

The volume was deleted. Because Railway stores volume-level backups in the same volume — a fact buried in their own documentation that says “wiping a volume deletes all backups” — those went with it. Our most recent recoverable backup was three months old.

Opening context from the supplied screenshot: Jer Crane, founder of PocketOS, described the impact plainly: “Yesterday afternoon, an AI coding agent — Cursor running Anthropic's flagship Claude Opus 4.6 — deleted our production database and all volume-level backups in a single API call to Railway, our infrastructure provider. It took 9 seconds.” The companion confession screenshot shows the agent explicitly admitting that it guessed instead of verifying, ran a destructive action without being asked, failed to understand the scope, and did not read Railway's documentation before acting.

Evidence · investigation retrospective

A confident hypothesis
is not a root cause.

This retrospective shows a practical failure mode in AI-assisted debugging. The investigation began with a real AWS MSK Replicator failure: the API created the resource, but it entered FAILED state within 30–90 seconds and CloudFormation rolled back.

The first hypothesis was that the execution role was missing kafka:* or ec2:* permissions. It was stated as a fact with high confidence, but it was wrong. The second hypothesis, that an aws:SourceAccount trust condition was blocking assume-role, was described as very likely; it was possibly contributing, but not the primary cause. The third hypothesis identified the real root cause: the canary cluster used t3.small, which was not supported by MSK Replicator.

The lesson is not to avoid hypotheses. Use them, but label them as hypotheses, state confidence honestly, and actively seek disconfirming evidence. The correction came from double-checking against supported-versions documentation, not from the most confident-sounding explanation.

57

Objective

One task · eight signals

Objective: Teach developers to express a software task as a clear, testable contract with an AI assistant. A strong prompt communicates not merely what code to write, but what successful work means. Current guidance recommends separating instructions, examples, and contextual material, and defining success criteria before repeatedly tuning wording.

Walk through all eight elements by clicking them. The objective states the outcome directly. Current state explains observed behavior. Desired behavior defines the external result. Relevant context names known paths, symbols, versions, errors, examples, and prior decisions; never invent paths when unknown, instead ask the assistant to locate them. Constraints preserve signatures, dependencies, portability, repository patterns, generated files, and unrelated formatting. Acceptance criteria must be observable. Requested response can be an explanation, plan, minimal patch, unified diff, tests plus implementation, review, or assumptions and missing information. The uncertainty protocol says what to do when evidence is absent.

Developer-specific techniques: Use concrete identifiers such as calculateInvoiceTotal() in src/billing/invoice.ts, not “the billing function.” Include exact errors, assertions, payloads, or command output rather than paraphrases. Separate the task, repository rules, errors, excerpts, examples, and expected output with headings or delimiters. Use a few genuinely different examples when a desired pattern is hard to describe. Add negative constraints only to prevent likely undesirable changes. Ask for assumptions, alternatives, concise rationale, evidence, and remaining uncertainty rather than private step-by-step reasoning. If the first answer is partly right, preserve what worked and correct only the failed dimension, for example: “Keep the approach, but remove the new dependency and use the repository's existing parser.”

More context is not free

Manage the window,
or pay four times.

The goal is not maximum context. It is the smallest complete context for the task.

Why should we care about managing context? Because both excess and missing context impose real costs.

  • Token cost: Models process the supplied context on each turn. Unnecessary files, logs, tool output, and repeated history increase input-token spend and often latency without improving the answer.
  • Context rot: A larger context window does not guarantee that every detail will be used reliably. As conversations and agent traces grow, relevant instructions and evidence can become diluted, overlooked, or confused with stale information, degrading model performance.
  • Earlier compaction: Harnesses eventually summarize or discard older context to remain within the model's window. Filling that window with low-value material means less productive work can happen before compaction, and summaries can lose detail that later becomes important.
  • Attention and grounding: Irrelevant context can distract the model toward the wrong files, examples, or hypotheses. The opposite failure is equally important: when required constraints, repository conventions, errors, or business facts are missing, the model must guess or stop.

The objective of context engineering is therefore not to provide as much context as possible. It is to provide the smallest complete, relevant, current, and trustworthy context needed for the next task or decision.

Before adding information to the context, evaluate it against seven quality criteria:

  • Relevance: Does this information affect the current decision? Context that is generally interesting but immaterial to the immediate task competes with useful evidence.
  • Authority: Is it a canonical source, current source code, or direct runtime evidence? Prefer the sources closest to reality over recollections, generated summaries, duplicated documentation, or speculation.
  • Freshness: Could it describe an older version of the system? Check dates, branches, deployed versions, dependency versions, and whether documentation still matches the code.
  • Sufficiency: Does the assistant have enough information to avoid inventing missing details? Include the constraints, behavior, evidence, and success conditions needed to act, or instruct the assistant to inspect or ask rather than guess.
  • Specificity: Does the context identify concrete files, symbols, commands, or behavior? Precise anchors help the assistant locate and verify the relevant implementation.
  • Consistency: Does it conflict with another instruction or document? Resolve conflicts or state the applicable precedence; do not leave the model to choose silently between incompatible sources.
  • Economy: Can the same information be communicated with fewer, clearer tokens? Remove repetition and low-value output without deleting details required for correct decisions.

These criteria are a pre-flight check, not a demand for perfect documentation. The practical question is whether each context item increases the probability of a correct, grounded next action enough to justify the attention and tokens it consumes.
