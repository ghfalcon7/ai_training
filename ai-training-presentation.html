This is a simplified map and the fields overlap in practice.

  • Artificial intelligence: the broad field of systems performing tasks associated with human intelligence: perception, language, reasoning, learning, or decision-making. A virtual assistant combines several of these.
  • Machine learning: systems learn patterns from examples rather than having every rule explicitly programmed. Example: spam filtering.
  • The map also includes supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning; deep learning; computer vision; speech processing; generative AI; image/video models; LLMs; robotics; planning/search; and expert systems.

  • Supervised learning: labeled examples include the input and correct answer. Emails labeled spam or safe teach classification.
  • Unsupervised learning: no predefined labels. Example customer features include purchases/year, average order, categories, return rate, and recency. Customers A (45 purchases, $25, household) and B (42, $30, household) cluster together; C (3, $600, electronics) forms another cluster. Humans select features, inspect/name clusters, judge usefulness, and choose action. “Frequent small-basket shoppers” might get loyalty subscriptions; “occasional premium buyers” might get premium recommendations. The algorithm does not inherently understand “frequent” or “premium.”
  • Reinforcement learning: an agent receives rewards and penalties; a game agent learns moves that increase win probability.
  • Deep learning: neural networks with many layers learn complex representations from large data, such as visual cat features learned from thousands of images instead of handwritten rules.

  • Computer vision: extracts meaning from images/video, for example pedestrian and traffic-sign detection.
  • Speech processing: recognizes, understands, or produces speech, for example meeting transcription and speaker identification.
  • Generative AI: creates new content from learned patterns rather than only classifying or analyzing; examples include illustrations or code drafts.
  • Image/video models: generate or transform visual content, such as interface illustrations from a prompt.
  • LLMs: models trained on text and code to predict token sequences; coding assistants explain functions, propose patches, and generate tests.
  • Robotics: combines AI, sensors, and machines so decisions cause physical action; a warehouse robot senses shelves, plans routes, avoids people, and carries packages.
  • Planning/search: explores possible states/actions toward a goal, such as route selection or chess search.
  • Expert systems: explicit human-written rules, e.g. IF temperature high AND coolant pressure low THEN warn. Predictable and explainable, but narrow and expensive to maintain.

An LLM-powered software engineering agent can receive a goal, inspect a codebase, plan a solution, use development tools, edit files, run tests, evaluate results, and iterate. The LLM contributes language understanding, code generation, and reasoning. The surrounding agentic system contributes context, tools, memory, permissions, and the execution loop needed to act.

Fundamentals
08

Under the assistant:
next-token prediction

Repeated across enormous amounts of text and code.

“The developer fixed the bug and ran the tests”

LLMs are neural networks based on the Transformer architecture. During training they repeatedly predict the next token. Predicting well forces them to learn statistical representations of grammar, concepts, facts, relationships, code structure, and common reasoning patterns.

Mental model
09

encode patterns

Post-training then teaches instruction-following and assistant behavior.

Lossy compression

The model's knowledge is encoded statistically across billions of parameters. It is closer to a lossy compression of patterns in human knowledge than a verified database. Post-training teaches it to follow instructions and behave like an assistant. For a deeper accessible explanation, see Andrej Karpathy's Intro to Large Language Models.

Current limitations
11

Capability is uneven

Plausible
≠ correct
HallucinationLimited groundingFinite contextNon-determinism Prompt sensitivityLong-task driftUnreliable confidenceBiasOutdated knowledgeSecurity risks Verification gaps
  • Hallucination, largely intrinsic: next-token prediction rewards plausible language, not verified truth; a model can invent a library method and convincing docs.
  • Limited grounding, intrinsic to text-only training: words describe reality, but the model has not physically experienced it.
  • Finite context, architectural/operational: only available context can be reasoned over, and included details may still be overlooked.
  • Non-determinism, usually intentional: sampling means the same prompt can yield correct or faulty SQL.
  • Prompt sensitivity, largely intrinsic: “fix this” may rewrite broadly; “smallest change passing this test” may target precisely.
  • Long-task drift, partly intrinsic/systemic: generated actions become later context, early errors compound, and no inherent persistent goal state exists.
  • Unreliable confidence: token probability measures plausible wording, not truth; confident regex claims may fail Unicode.
  • Training-data errors/bias: imperfect human text/code can promote outdated insecure patterns.
  • Outdated knowledge, not fundamental: weights are mostly fixed after training; retrieval/tools can address it.
  • Specification dependence: “make login faster” may remove implicit security controls.
  • Verification gaps: passing tests cannot validate untested concurrency.
  • Security risks: instructions and untrusted content share a natural-language channel; malicious README/issue content may induce secret exfiltration. Restrict permissions.
  • Opaque reasoning: distributed numerical computation makes precise internal reasons difficult to inspect.
  • Cost/latency: repeated repository reads, tool calls, and full tests can cost minutes and millions of tokens.

Evidence 02
13
Limited grounding

Words are not
experience.

“Nearby + traffic” suggests walking. “Wash the car” requires the car.

The model recognizes relationships among “car,” “traffic,” “walking,” and “car wash,” but has never owned or washed a car. In the example, “nearby destination + heavy traffic” triggers “walk,” overlooking the physical purpose: the car needs washing, so the car must go there. Human concepts are grounded in physical experience, goals, and cause/effect; LLM concepts are primarily token relationships. This explains fluent discussion with occasional practically obvious failures.

Models can improve through better training data, larger or more efficient architectures, improved reasoning, longer context, multimodal input, stronger post-training, and better uncertainty calibration. Systems improve by adding repository context, retrieval, memory, specialized tools, automated verification, human review, and permission to abstain when evidence is insufficient.

The engineering contract

Ground important claims.
Verify consequential outputs.

Never turn fluent output into automatic trust.

EvidenceTestsReviewControls

Open-ended generation is optimized for plausible output rather than truth. Better models can reduce hallucinations, but unrestricted answers are never automatically correct. Reliable systems ground important claims in evidence and verify consequential outputs. Current LLMs also lack human experience, persistent goals, and consistently grounded physical/social understanding. Their capabilities are real and remarkable but uneven: a model may solve a difficult coding problem and fail a simple everyday situation.

The goal is not to know everything first.

This young field has significant potential impact and investment. Models, tools, and practices improve quickly, so developers should make a reasonable effort to stay informed to recognize opportunities, adapt, and remain competitive.

Do not follow every release, feature, benchmark, or social post. That creates anxiety, burnout, and FOMO, and much of today's excitement will be replaced or forgotten. Review periodically, experiment selectively, and focus on developments that remain useful after the initial hype. Reasonable occasional investment will catch advances that matter once the dust settles. The goal is not to know everything first; it is to recognize durable changes and apply them effectively when relevant.
